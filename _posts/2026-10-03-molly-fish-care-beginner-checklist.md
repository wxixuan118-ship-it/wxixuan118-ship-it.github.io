Molly fish care looks easy on the store label. Mollies are colorful, active and usually tagged "beginner friendly." Yet many new keepers lose them in the first few weeks, and the reason is rarely a mystery disease. It is usually the water: a tank that isn't cycled yet, or soft, acidic tap water that suits other community fish but not mollies.

This checklist walks through what to sort out before you buy, how to set up the tank, what to feed, and what to expect when your mollies start breeding (they will). ## Before you buy: check your water, not just the fish

The most useful molly care step happens before you visit the shop. Mollies (genus *Poecilia*) do best in warm, moderately hard, slightly alkaline water, and that is something you either have from the tap or have to build.

Test three things in your tap water:

- **pH**: a practical target for mollies is roughly 7.5–8.2.
- **GH (general hardness)**: tells you whether there are enough calcium and magnesium minerals.
- **KH (carbonate hardness)**: shows how well the water resists pH swings.

If your tap water is naturally soft and acidic, think twice. Constantly dosing buffers to chase a number is riskier than choosing a fish that likes your water. Stable water you can keep stable beats perfect water you can only hit occasionally.

The other pre-purchase task is cycling. A new tank needs time for beneficial bacteria to establish so that ammonia and nitrite stay at 0 ppm. Adding mollies to an uncycled tank is one of the most common reasons they die early.

## Molly tank size and setup

A 20-gallon aquarium is a practical minimum for a small group of common short-fin mollies. Go for 30 gallons or more if you want sailfin mollies, a mixed-sex group, or any chance of keeping fry. Adult mollies usually reach about 3–5 inches, and sailfins can grow larger, so plan for the adult fish rather than the juvenile in the bag.

Shape matters as much as volume. Mollies swim horizontally, so a long or standard tank works better than a tall, narrow one. If you are comparing tanks, a [tank size calculator](https://www.fishcareai.com/tools/aquarium-size-calculator/) helps turn dimensions into usable volume.

A basic molly setup includes:

1. **A mature filter** with enough biological media and some surface movement.
2. **An adjustable heater and a separate thermometer**, kept around 72–82°F (22–28°C). Stability matters more than the exact number.
3. **Plants along the sides and back**, with an open swimming lane in the middle. Floating plants give females and fry somewhere to hide.
4. **A lid**, plus decor without sharp edges that could tear long fins.

## Do mollies need salt?

Not routinely. Some wild mollies live in coastal or brackish water, which is where the "mollies need salt" advice comes from. Most store-bought mollies live well in properly mineralized freshwater.

Salt is not a fix for soft water. It raises salinity, but it doesn't add the calcium and magnesium that GH measures. Adding salt casually can also harm plants, snails, shrimp and soft-water fish in the same tank. If you want a brackish molly tank, plan it deliberately and measure salinity.

## Group size, tank mates and the male-to-female ratio

Mollies are social and do better in a group, often five or more when the tank is big enough. The catch is that males pursue females almost constantly. Keep at least two, and ideally three, females for every male to spread that attention out.

Good molly tank mates share warm, harder water and a peaceful temperament:

- Platies, swordtails and guppies (fellow livebearers)
- Bristlenose plecos
- Many peaceful rainbowfish, danios and gouramis, if the water suits them

Avoid fin-nippers such as tiger barbs, aggressive cichlids, goldfish (cooler water) and small shrimp, which mollies may eat. Before adding a new species, a [fish compatibility checker](https://www.fishcareai.com/tools/fish-compatibility-checker/) is a quick way to compare water ranges, size and temperament.

## What to feed mollies

Mollies are omnivores with a strong taste for plants. In the wild they graze on algae and biofilm as well as eating small invertebrates.

A simple molly diet:

- **Staple**: a quality flake or small pellet that lists spirulina or algae.
- **Plant extras**: spirulina wafers, blanched zucchini or spinach.
- **Protein variety**: frozen daphnia, baby brine shrimp or bloodworms two or three times a week.

Feed one to three small meals a day, only as much as the fish finish in a minute or two. Leftover food breaks down into ammonia, which hits a new tank hardest. Long strings of waste or cloudy water usually mean you are feeding too much.

## Molly breeding: plan for fry before you mix sexes

If you keep males and females together, you will get babies. Mollies are livebearers. Pregnancy usually lasts about 30–60 days, and a single brood can range from around 10 to more than 100 fry. Females can store sperm, so a female bought without a male may still give birth weeks later.

Signs of pregnancy include a rounder belly and, on lighter fish, a darkening spot near the anal fin. Late in pregnancy, the belly can look almost square. Don't mistake raised, pinecone-like scales for pregnancy; that is a health warning, not a birth sign.

Adults don't care for their fry and will eat them. Dense floating plants such as hornwort or water sprite give newborns cover. To raise more of them, move the female to a separate cycled tank before birth, feed the fry crushed flake or fry food three or four times a day, and decide early where the juveniles will go. Mollies breed quickly, and a tank can become overstocked within a few months.

## Warning signs to act on early

Molly problems usually show up in behavior first. Watch for:

- Shimmying: rocking in place with little forward movement
- Clamped fins, gasping at the surface or rapid breathing
- White spots, a dusty coating, frayed fins or swelling
- Hiding or refusing food

Shimmying is a symptom, not a disease, and it is often linked to water: low minerals, temperature swings, or ammonia and nitrite. Test the water before reaching for medication. If symptoms persist, a [molly health problems guide](https://www.fishcareai.com/fish-health/fish/molly) can help you narrow down likely causes, and an aquatic vet is the right call for serious or unexplained illness.

## FAQ

### Are molly fish good for beginners?

They can be, if the beginner is ready for their needs: a cycled tank of at least 20 gallons, stable warm water with adequate hardness, and a plan for fry. They are less forgiving in soft, acidic water or new tanks.

### How long do molly fish live?

Usually about three to five years. Water quality, stress, genetics and the fish's condition when you bought it all affect how long an individual molly lives.

### How many mollies should I keep together?

Aim for a small group, often five or more if the tank allows, with two or three females per male. Don't squeeze a big group into a small tank just to reach a number.

## Conclusion

Good molly fish care comes down to a few habits: match the fish to your water, cycle the tank first, give them room to swim, keep the male-to-female ratio sensible and plan for fry before they arrive. Get those right and mollies are lively, long-lived community fish.
